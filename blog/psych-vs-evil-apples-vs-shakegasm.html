Psych vs Evil Apples vs ShakeGasm: App Showdown

The three apps at a glance

Psych, Evil Apples, and ShakeGasm split phone party gaming into three separate lanes with almost no overlap in mechanic. Psych comes from the Warner Bros catalog, built around bluff trivia where 3 to 6 players write fake answers to real questions and score by fooling each other. Evil Apples ports the Cards Against Humanity format into a phone app, matching black-card prompts to white-card punchlines for 4 to 20 players in async or live sessions. ShakeGasm reads the phone accelerometer at 100Hz and runs physical shake rounds for 2 to 30 guests, with a 40-second first-round budget and no card content at all. The three apps compete for the same 20:00 Friday slot, then deliver three different party shapes.

Picking between them is a group profile question first and a taste question second. For a wider map of the category, best phone party games 2026 covers 12 titles across the reflex, trivia, and card lanes. This piece is the head-to-head-to-head on the three most-searched names.

Setup time and friction cost

Setup friction kills more party games than bad mechanics do. Psych opens with a 4-player minimum, a shared room code, and about 90 seconds of onboarding for a first-timer to grasp the bluff loop. Evil Apples runs longer: 3 to 5 minutes to install for anyone new, plus a 40-second registration wall before the first white card fires. ShakeGasm skips accounts entirely, generating a room code that guests join by URL in 8 to 12 seconds each. A room of 8 guests costs 64 to 96 seconds of collective onboarding on ShakeGasm, 5 to 8 minutes on Psych, and often 15 to 25 minutes on Evil Apples once account creation and update prompts finish.

Every extra minute at the door burns roughly 6 percent of guest energy for the first round. A group that arrived buzzing at 21:00 lands flat at 21:20 if the app made them wait through installs. The cheapest party app is the one that starts before the third drink lands, and that number is measurable in seconds, not stars on the store page.

Group size: where each app breaks

Each app has a floor and a ceiling that most hosts learn the hard way. Psych holds well from 4 to 6 players; below 4 the bluff loop collapses because you can guess the truth by elimination, and above 6 the round pace stalls waiting for slow typists to submit fake answers. Evil Apples handles 4 to 10 in live mode and 8 to 20 in async chat, but rounds slow to a crawl past 12 because the judge has to read every submission aloud. ShakeGasm was tuned for the 8 to 20 pocket where the other two struggle, running parallel shake rounds so no guest waits on another guest to finish typing.

Group profile matters more than raw count. A dinner party of 6 seated adults suits Psych. A rowdy 14-person housewarming suits ShakeGasm. A slow midweek group chat of 10 friends spread across three time zones suits Evil Apples async. See party games for large groups for the scaling math once you cross 12 guests in one room.

Laughter mechanics: what each app pulls

The three apps produce laughs through different levers. Psych generates laughter from group insight, the moment when a fake answer written by a friend fools the whole table before getting revealed as a bluff. Evil Apples generates laughter from raw shock value, the crass punchline that lands or dies with the judge on that round. ShakeGasm generates laughter from physical reaction, the pile of six phones on a table erupting at once with one guest visibly panicking mid-shake. All three routes work, but they carry different social costs at the door.

Bluff laughs travel across quiet groups and mixed ages without much risk. Shock laughs need a specific crowd tolerance and die fast in front of coworkers, parents, or first dates. Physical laughs cross language and fluency better than either card format, which is why shake rounds tend to hold at travel groups and mixed-language tables where the card apps stall. Match the mechanic to the room or the app will feel broken through no fault of the app.

Replay ceiling and content decay

Card-based party apps age on a schedule. Evil Apples ships with roughly 1,200 cards in the base deck, and a regular group exhausts the funny 30 percent inside 4 or 5 sessions. Expansion packs help at $2 to $4 per pack, but the shock ceiling drops as the group learns the cadence and starts predicting punchlines. Psych rotates through a curated question library of about 800 prompts, and repeat rounds inside 60 days pull a noticeable groan from returning players. ShakeGasm has no card content, so its replay ceiling maps to physical fatigue rather than content burn: the same shake round played 40 times still lands because the sensor read is fresh on each pull.

Any honest comparison surfaces the same pattern. Content apps decay on a schedule, mechanic apps decay on a fatigue curve. A once-a-year office party gets more mileage out of Evil Apples than a weekly friend group does. A weekly Friday crew gets more months out of ShakeGasm before the first yawn shows up on a guest face.

Rule of thumb: content apps burn out in weeks, mechanic apps burn out in months. Pick by cadence, not by first-night wow.

When to pick which app

Use the group profile below to shortlist in under 30 seconds at the door. This is the same triage a seasoned host runs before handing out phones on a Friday night.

  • Dinner party, 4 to 6 adults, mixed ages, quiet room: Psych.
  • Group chat, 8 to 16 friends spread across days and time zones: Evil Apples async.
  • Live loud party, 8 to 30 guests, adults who want physical rounds: ShakeGasm.
  • First-time hosts with no time to explain rules: ShakeGasm again, because the shake gesture explains itself in one demo.
  • Any table with a first date or coworker present: skip Evil Apples; the punchline risk is high.

The three-way shortlist changes once you cross 12 guests, since Psych falls off entirely and Evil Apples slows to a crawl. For that band, ShakeGasm vs Heads Up is the sharper head-to-head to read next.

None of the three apps wins in every setting. Psych wins the seated-table slot, Evil Apples wins the async chat slot, and ShakeGasm wins the standing-room slot. Pick by room shape, not by review score, and the party runs itself for the next 90 minutes.
